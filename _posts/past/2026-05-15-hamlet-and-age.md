Were this a paper, I propose for it to be named ‘The Unbearable Lightness of Being Thirty: Age as Fact and Problem in *Hamlet*, ‘Prufrock’, and *Endgame*’. But that is neither here nor there. 

The question of how old Hamlet is (or rather, what it means to ask how old he is) recurs across the critical tradition with a persistence that suggests something more than antiquarianism is at stake. A. C. Bradley wondered; A. A. Jack demurred; the gravedigger provided a number (thirty); and generations of critics have either accepted that number, explained it away, or argued that the question itself misunderstands how literary time operates. *Hamlet* is a play that feels as though it should belong to youth (a prince whose filial duty collides with a corrupt court, whose university education leaves him overmatched by realpolitik, whose ‘antic disposition’ carries the performative texture of adolescent rebellion). And yet the text insists, in its most deliberately timed scene, that the prince is thirty years old. In early modern England, as Harkins notes, thirty was the farthest reach of what could plausibly be called ‘youth’ (a liminal age).[[1\]](#_ftn1) Hamlet is thus both too old for the young revenger the genre demands and too politically positioned as ‘young’ to claim the authority Claudius denies him—whose usurpation depends upon producing Hamlet as a youth (‘an understanding simple and unschool’d’). Hamlet’s tragedy is not simply that he cannot act, but that he cannot escape the category through which the court renders him incapable of action.

The modernist afterlife of *Hamlet* begins when this political predicament becomes a structure of consciousness. T. S. Eliot’s ‘The Love Song of J. Alfred Prufrock’ imagines the Hamlet-figure who survives Elsinore (by going back to Wittenberg, growing old, and becoming ‘an attendant lord … almost ridiculous’). ‘Do I dare?’ restages ‘To be or not to be?’ after the revenge plot has disappeared and existential crisis has collapsed into social paralysis. Tragically, Prufrock has the capacity to act only in roles that do not quite matter: ‘deferential, glad to be of use’ (swelling a progress, advising the real prince).  

Beckett’s *Endgame* takes this logic further. Hamm is Hamlet after the revenge plot has exhausted itself (after Denmark has become ‘zero’ and the sea ‘lead’). There is no kingdom left to inherit, no future toward which action could move (the revenge plot cannot even begin). Hence Hamm’s ‘Mean something! You and I, mean something!’ Across these texts, age marks the accumulation of time without the accumulation of meaning: the condition of having lived long enough for the great Hamlet-questions (‘To be or not to be?’ ‘Do I dare?’) to persist after the worlds that once gave them consequence have disappeared.
