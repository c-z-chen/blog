description: How do Hamlet and Goethe’s Wilhelm Meister present family as a historical fact, an existential condition, and/or a political problem?
---

*Hamlet* and Goethe’s *Wilhelm Meister* are of course composed in different centuries, different national contexts, and in different genres, yet both share one insight, that the family is a constructed relation that transmits not only property and names but also curses, debts, and the fundamental uncertainty of whom one may be. In Hamlet, the family is a scene of violent succession (a political institution whose collapse radiates outward to disorder the entire state). Anselm Haverkamp, in ‘The Ghost of History’, introduces the word ‘mortgage’, borrowed from French during Shakespeare’s time, originally referring to ‘the money that we owe to the dead, those to whom we remain permanently indebted without need of repayment’.[[1\]](#_ftn1) One might recall our discussion in class that the ghost does not just demand revenge, but also demand that Hamlet remember. To remember, it would seem, is to assume a debt that can never be discharged. Indeed, the register of inheritance (property law) is established at the very opening (‘Did slay this Fortinbras; who, … Did forfeit, with his life, all those his lands’). In Wilhelm Meister, the eponymous protagonist’s grandfather possessed a ‘beautiful collection of pictures, drawings, copper-plates, and antiquities’ (a treasure sold by Wilhelm’s father and converted into commercial capital). Wilhelm refuses both the grandfather’s inheritance of culture (*Bildung* in its substantive sense) and the father’s inheritance of commerce (*Bildung* as professional formation), since the stage offers Wilhelm the only space where a burgher can seem (where personal carriage can equal inherited status), and he diagnoses the family as an economic institution that transmits function. The novel further engages with inheritance through Mignon and the harper. The harper carries a crime (an implied incestuous relation) that he cannot discharge, the living figure of *mortgage*. Mignon, too, is an orphan of uncertain origin (‘the Great Devil’s’ child, who sings of Italy, a homeland she has lost before she could possess it). The family is also presented as grave political problem. One of *Hamlet*’s most famous line (‘The time is out of joint. O cursed spite, / That ever I was born to set it right!’), it would seem, is simultaneously a political diagnosis and an existential lament. The ‘joint’ that is out of place is the same ‘joint’ that makes Gertrude a ‘jointress’. The family’s disordering (the murder of a king, the incestuous marriage of his brother to his widow) has disordered the state itself. As Richard Dutton points out, the play’s addition of Fortinbras (who appears in no prior version of the story) changes the intra-family revenge plot into a meditation on regime change.[[2\]](#_ftn2) Where *Hamlet* ends with annihilation (the royal line extinct, a foreign prince seizing the throne), *Wilhelm Meister* ends with freedom (‘Nature has pronounced thee free’); only this freedom must be understood as the capacity to carry the past (Mignon’s funeral in the ‘Saal der Vergangenheit’) without being crushed by it.

[[2\]](#_ftnref2) See Richard Dutton, ‘Hamlet and succession’, in *Doubtful and dangerous*, ed. Susan Doran and Paulina Kewes (Manchester: Manchester University Press, 2014), 173–91. Dutton’s textual analysis of Q1 (1603), Q2 (1604/5), and F (1623) shows how the play was revised to address the Jacobean succession. Q2 (‘enlarged to almost as much againe as it was’) adds the naming of Claudius (the Roman emperor who married his niece Agrippina, mother of Nero), the portents before Julius Caesar’s fall, and the soliloquy in which Hamlet contrasts himself with Fortinbras (4.4). These additions, Dutton argues, were made for court performance before James I (the Scottish king who united two crowns, as Fortinbras will unite Norway and Denmark).
