It is an old and internationally ratified article of Common Sense (not to say & the Rights of Man), that a sharp blade should decline into bluntness through idleness—often metaphorically developed to suggest that a sharp brain should lapse into dullness through disuse. One’s liver, a regular lump of flesh much like the old brain—sharp, I trust, much like the old blade—is surely to sink into a species of moribund stagnation, growing, as it were, deader instead of liver, when denied its proper and spirited engagements; and mine, I am pleased, indeed compelled, to report, has seldom, if ever, been otherwise than livelier.

One brings to mind a certain Ode to The Bottle, a *Laude Virtutis Vini*, two centuries before Horace, by the poet Liu Ling (刘伶):

> 有大人先生，以天地为一朝，万期为须臾。日月为扃牖，八荒为庭衢。行无辙迹，居无室庐。幕天席地，纵意所如。止则操卮执觚，动则挈榼提壶。唯酒是务，焉知其馀。有贵介公子，搢绅处士。闻吾风声，议其所以。乃奋袂攘襟，怒目切齿。陈说礼法，是非锋起。先生于是方捧罂承槽，衔杯漱醪。奋髯踑踞，枕麹藉糟。无思无虑，其乐陶陶。兀然而醉，豁尔而醒。静听不闻雷霆之声，熟视不睹泰山之形。不觉寒暑之切肌，利欲之感情。俯观万物，扰扰焉如江汉之载浮萍。二豪侍侧，焉如蜾蠃之与螟蛉。

As the Dude finely puts it: is that some kind of an eastern thing? Yes, and I translate it to mean:

> There is a Great Man, a Gentleman, who holds the Heavens and Earth for one singular dawn, and the ten-thousand terms for a mere instant. The sun and moon are folding doors and lattice windows; the eight wastes are courts and thoroughfares. Going, he leaves no rut nor trace; dwelling, he has no chamber nor hut. The Heavens as curtain, the Earth as mat, his wills whithersoever it pleases he pursues. Resting, he grasps a cup and holds a goblet; stirring, he carries a flagon and lifts a tankard. Only unto wine is he devoted; aught of the rest, who knows? There are splendid scions, and grave girdled-gown gentlemen of retirement. Hearing the drift of my repute, they debate the wherefores of it. Thereupon they shake out sleeves, thrust up robes, glare with grim eyes, gnash with grinding teeth. They set forth and declare rites and statutes; rights and wrongs rise keen-edged. The Gentleman thereupon is just then clasping an earthen jar and receiving the flowing vat; with cup in mouth he rinses with the lees. Beard bristling, he squats astride; the yeast his pillow, he reclines upon the dregs. By no thought, by no care, his joy is deep and full. In a stupor, he is drunk; all at once, wide and clear he awakes. Stilly he listens, and hearkens not the sound of thunder; fixedly he looks, and sees not the form of Mount Tai. He feels not the keen bite of cold and heat upon his flesh, nor the stirring of profit and desire upon his affections. Bending, he beholds all mortal things, troubling and thronging in their restless turmoil, as if they were duckweed borne upon the Yangtze and the Han Rivers. The two grand heroes standing attendant at his side, are to him as the wasp and the caterpillar’s young.

In that authoritative compilation, *The Book of Jin* (《晋书》), being the officially sanctioned historical record of that dynasty, there is extant a notice concerning our present poet Liu Ling, to the effect that he was habitually conveyed abroad in a vehicle drawn by a deer, having in his possession a vessel of wine, and being moreover attended by an individual bearing a spade, to whom he was accustomed to deliver the following instruction: that, in the eventuality of his decease, interment should be effected *in situ*, without removal. What amplitude of spirit! What grace! What victory!—Should death supervene in the act intoxication, at least any of those dread customary solicitudes of mortality would be obviated.

If one compares Liu Ling with the modern man, the contrast would not be found to the advantage of the latter. For people nowadays—they drink until their faces become suffused to either beet-red or paper-pale, their necks distended, and until their convivial exertions conclude in a mutual sustentation of bodies scarcely capable of independent locomotion, followed by recourse to remedial procedures of a decidedly unheroic character. It may therefore be affirmed (& without undue exaggeration) that there has been effected a diminution, if not an extinction, of that disposition of mind which rendered possible the conduct attributed to Liu Ling.

But then again, one suspects that it is not improbable that Liu Ling, notwithstanding the apparent extremity of his declarations, entertained an implicit confidence in the improbability of their literal realisation; which consideration would sufficiently account for the facility with which they were boldly proclaimed (historical records indicate that his death occurred in the ordinary course of senescence). One suspects that in the circumstance of an actual approach to death by intoxication, the individual concerned would not care to consider his burial. Indeed, as Su dongpo has it: 

>  常怪刘伶死更埋，岂伊忘死未忘骸。
>  乌鸢夺得与蝼蚁，谁信先生无此怀。
