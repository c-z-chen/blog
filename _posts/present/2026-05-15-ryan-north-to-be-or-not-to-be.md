What does it really mean to be winning? In Ryan North’s *To Be or Not to Be*, the acquisition of triumph is attended by no especial labour. One may, as the volume (with almost indecorous alacrity) proposes, kill Claudius immediately after the spectral speech, ascend the throne, and administer the realm with so provident and luminously paternal a beneficence that the entire posterior moiety of the drama suffers instantaneous annulment. One may likewise, under the designation of Ophelia, invent central heating, provoke Fortinbras to a circumterrestrial footrace, or (an eventuality toward which North appears animated by peculiar partiality) become a muscle-bound scientist who needs no man’s rescue. None of these proceedings is difficult. The book is positively importunate in its solicitation that the reader should win. It has, as they say in the trade, a low frustration threshold. On my run, I got to page 500, a marvellous broadsheet parody, The Hereafter Herald, with the headline ‘Earl Hamlet honoured yet again / Reformed ghost awarded “Spirit of Spiriting” title for third year in a row.’ (And another headline that says, of course, Hitchens is still refusing to believe in heaven.) The ghost of Hamlet’s father (the figure who sets the entire tragedy in motion, the voice from beyond that commands revenge) is rehabilitated, and learnt to redirect his spiriting propensities into productive civic engagement (he has, in the lingo of twelve-step programs, made amends). Yet by what conceivable metaphysic may a ghost be said to make amends? The ghost’s dramatic office is to be irreconcilable, to appear from Purgatory (or from a place that looks like Purgatory, smells like Purgatory, and quacks like Purgatory) to demand of his son the perpetration of homicide. The ‘Spirit of Spiriting’ award! A spirit that spirits responsibly, that goes above and beyond its civic duty—is it a spirit still? North’s book abounds in endings of conspicuous utility and hygienic optimism (Ophelia invents central heating, Hamlet becomes a pirate; everyone lives, everyone wins). But the play we love, the one that gives the airy nothing of revenge tragedy a local habitation and a name, is the play where no one wins. The play where the ghost is irreconcilable! The play where Ophelia drowns! The play where Hamlet, in the Second Quarto’s final words, says ‘Let be’. Not ‘Let me win.’ Not ‘Let me choose.’ Let be!
