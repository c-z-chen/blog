I have very roughly read Sally Rooney's *Conversations with Friends* for a class; I cannot say I enjoyed it, but I found it interesting as regards the following aspects.

I suspect that much of my speculations are perhaps wrong—I am not much too familiar with the history or the ideology of communism (I have only read Mao Zedong’s selected essays, and a few other Chinese or Soviet communist novels here and there), and is certainly unfit to contemplate it academically. I speculate however that it is potentially profitable to read the novel as some sort of a communist novel. Rooney is of course by no means a Soviet novelist (she was born in the year the Soviet Union collapsed!). My limited research seems to suggest that she is overall a leftist in politics, but her imagination, one speculates, is (at least by the time she wrote *Conversations with Friends*) shaped by capitalism (she grew up in the Celtic Tiger, and then the 2008 crash).

I had quite forgotten that Frances is a communist (though every now and then I am reminded, I did not think the slogan bore too much importance) until France’s confession (‘I wasn’t trying to trash your life, I was trying to steal it’). This to an extent to me is a reflection of the situation of communism in the world as it is now. Indeed, the revolutionary impulse to destroy capitalism has been replaced by a melancholic desire to have what capitalism has. The Soviet Union is dead (and there is at least one other example I know). What remains is not a revolutionary project (but instead a longing): to steal the bourgeois life, to possess the cashmere coat, to live in the house on Monkstown. Frances is, one is afraid, certainly not Rosa Luxemburg (she is not even a reformist). She is a person who has internalised the logic of capitalism to such a degree that the only way she can imagine escaping her class position is to take what belongs to someone else.

The Soviet project, at its revolutionary inception, was in my limited understanding about trashing the bourgeois world (abolishing private property, seizing the means of production, destroying the old social order). By the time of its collapse, what remained was a corrupted system (bureaucratic, authoritarian, economically stagnant). After the collapse, in the former Soviet republics, there emerged a desperate, often humiliating embrace of capitalism (oligarchs, privatisation, the worship of Western goods). The post-Soviet subject, one might argue, no longer wants to trash capitalism. They want to steal it (to get their share, to own a dacha, to wear Italian shoes, to send their children to study in London). Frances, our declared communist, might just be this subject!

And the novel’s tragedy (if it is a tragedy) is perhaps that Frances knows this. She knows that she is a thief; she wants to nick, and she wants Nick! Indeed, Mao Zedong warned of the sugar-coated cannonballs of capitalism: ‘There may be such and such communists, who have not been subjected by the enemies and their guns, who do not fail their names as heroes before the enemies; but they cannot withstand the assault of sugar-coated cannonballs, and suffers defeat when confronted by these sugared projectiles.’ (可能有这样一些共产党人，他们是不曾被拿枪的敌人征服过的，他们在这些敌人面前不愧英雄的称号；但是经不起人们用糖衣裹着的炮弹攻击，他们在糖弹面前要打败仗, translation mine).

‘You can’t always take the analytical position.’ At a certain point, analysis stops (live, laugh, love!—in the lingo of our times). Perhaps, I am going too far.
